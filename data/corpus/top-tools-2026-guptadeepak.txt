Top 9 Project Management Tools of 2026: Linear vs Notion vs Jira vs the Rest
Project management tools compared: Linear, Notion, Asana, Monday.com, ClickUp, Jira, Basecamp, Trello, and Coda.
The right project management tool depends more on team size and workflow style than any single feature list. A 5-person startup and a 500-person engineering org need genuinely different tools, not the same tool with more seats. This comparison ranks Linear, Notion, Asana, Monday.com, ClickUp, Jira, Basecamp, Trello, and Coda on pricing, team fit, scalability, and workflow trade-offs.
If your team is choosing between an all-in-one workspace and a dedicated PM tool, see note-taking and PKM apps compared for the workspace side of that decision.

Linear
Best Overall
Best for: Engineering and product teams that value speed
“Linear is what Jira would be if it were rebuilt from scratch in 2024 with no legacy baggage. Every interaction feels instantaneous. Keyboard shortcuts cover every action. The opinionated workflow (triage, backlog, sprint, done) forces good habits without the configuration overhead that buries teams in Jira. For engineering and product teams under 200 people, it is the best tool available.”
Pros
- Sub-100ms response times on every interaction make it the fastest project management tool by a wide margin
- Keyboard-first design with shortcuts for every action means power users rarely touch the mouse
- Opinionated workflows (cycles, triage, backlog) enforce good practices without requiring an admin to configure them
Cons
- Rigid workflow model does not accommodate non-engineering use cases (marketing campaigns, content calendars, client projects) well
- Limited reporting and dashboarding compared to Jira or Asana for management visibility across many teams
Speed as a Feature
Linear's performance is not incremental. It is a different experience. Actions complete in under 100 milliseconds because the app uses an offline-first architecture with local data sync, similar to how Figma handles real-time collaboration. This speed changes behavior: teams that avoided their project management tool because it was slow (a common Jira complaint) actually use Linear daily. The command palette (Cmd+K) provides instant access to any project, issue, or action, and the keyboard shortcut system is the most complete of any PM tool tested.
Opinionated Workflow
Linear prescribes a specific workflow: issues enter triage, move to backlog, get scheduled into cycles (2-week sprints by default), and progress through states to completion. This opinionation is a strength for teams that want structure without spending weeks configuring it, and a limitation for teams whose work does not fit this model. Projects group related issues across cycles with progress tracking and target dates. Roadmaps provide a higher-level view for leadership. The system works exceptionally well for product-led engineering teams and poorly for agencies, consulting firms, or teams managing ongoing operations.
Integration Ecosystem
Linear integrates with GitHub, GitLab, Slack, Figma, and Sentry out of the box. The GitHub integration is particularly strong: PRs automatically link to issues, branch names are auto-generated from issue identifiers, and merging a PR can close the associated issue. The Slack integration creates issues from messages and provides status updates in channels. The API is well-documented and supports building custom integrations, though the marketplace of third-party integrations is smaller than Jira's or Asana's.
$8/user/month
Notion
Runner Up
Best for: All-in-one workspace combining projects, docs, and wiki
“Notion is the most flexible tool on this list, which is both its greatest strength and its most common failure mode. It can function as a project tracker, document hub, team wiki, CRM, and content calendar simultaneously. Teams that invest time in setting up their workspace get enormous value. Teams that skip setup end up with a disorganized collection of pages that nobody can find.”
Pros
- Combines project management, documentation, and wiki into a single tool, eliminating the need for separate subscriptions
- Database views (table, board, timeline, calendar, gallery) let you display the same data in whatever format suits the task
- Notion AI assists with writing, summarization, and task creation directly within the workspace
Cons
- Performance degrades noticeably with large databases (1,000+ items), especially on mobile
- Flexibility creates a blank-canvas problem: teams without a clear structure end up with disorganized, unsearchable workspaces
Flexibility and Structure
Notion's core concept is that everything is a block (text, database, embed, toggle, callout), and blocks compose into pages, which compose into workspaces. Databases are special blocks that support multiple views (table, kanban board, timeline, calendar, gallery) and relations between databases. This means you can build a project tracker that links to a client database that links to a contracts database, all within the same tool. Templates help teams get started quickly, but the long-term success of a Notion workspace depends on establishing and enforcing structural conventions early.
Notion AI Integration
Notion AI operates within the workspace context, which gives it an advantage over standalone AI tools. It can summarize meeting notes into action items, draft project briefs from linked databases, generate status updates from task completion data, and answer questions about your workspace content. The AI features are most useful for reducing the administrative overhead of project management (writing updates, summarizing discussions, creating templates) rather than for decision-making. At $10/member/month as an add-on, the value depends heavily on how much writing and summarization your team does.
Scaling Considerations
Notion works beautifully for teams of 5-50. Between 50 and 200, it requires intentional architecture and a workspace owner who manages permissions, templates, and organizational structure. Beyond 200, the lack of enterprise features (granular permissions, audit logs on lower tiers, compliance certifications) and performance limitations become real constraints. Large organizations often use Notion for documentation and wiki while running a dedicated PM tool (Linear, Jira) for task management.
Free (personal), $8/month Plus, $15/user/month Business
Asana
Honorable Mention
Best for: Marketing and operations teams managing cross-functional work
“Asana is the most polished project management tool for non-engineering teams. The Timeline view handles dependencies and scheduling better than any competitor. Workload balancing shows who is over-committed before deadlines slip. For marketing teams, operations groups, and cross-functional organizations, Asana provides structure without requiring everyone to think in sprints and story points.”
Pros
- Timeline view with dependency management provides genuine critical-path visibility that most competitors only approximate
- Workload view shows team capacity across projects, making over-allocation visible before it causes missed deadlines
- Free tier for up to 15 users is the most generous among serious PM tools
Cons
- Per-user pricing gets expensive at scale: a 100-person team on Business tier costs $13,200/year
- The interface has grown complex over time, with features layered on in ways that overwhelm new users
Timeline and Dependencies
Asana's Timeline view is its strongest differentiator. Unlike kanban boards that show current status, Timeline shows when work will happen, how tasks depend on each other, and where the schedule is at risk. Drag a task to reschedule it and all dependent tasks shift automatically. This is particularly useful for marketing launches, product releases, and any work where sequencing matters. Most competitors offer a timeline or Gantt view, but Asana's implementation handles dependency chains, milestones, and cross-project dependencies more reliably.
Workload and Resource Management
The Workload view aggregates effort estimates across all projects for each team member, showing who has capacity and who is over-committed. This requires teams to estimate effort on tasks (hours or story points), which adds overhead but provides real value for managers who need to prevent burnout and balance work distribution. For organizations running multiple concurrent projects with shared resources, this visibility prevents the common failure mode where everyone is assigned to too many projects simultaneously.
Portfolio Management
Asana Portfolios provide a dashboard view across multiple projects, showing status, progress, and milestones in a single view. Combined with Goals (OKR-style objective tracking), this gives leadership visibility into how project work connects to organizational priorities. The reporting features are more developed than Linear or Notion, though less customizable than Jira. For program managers overseeing 10-50 concurrent projects, Portfolios replace the spreadsheet-based status tracking that most organizations default to.
Free (up to 15 users), $10.99/user/month Starter, $24.99/user/month Advanced
Monday.com
Honorable Mention
Best for: Visual project tracking with non-technical teams
“Monday.com is the most visually intuitive PM tool. Color-coded boards, status columns, and dashboard widgets make project status visible at a glance without clicking into individual tasks. It excels with teams that include non-technical stakeholders who need to see progress without learning a complex tool.”
Pros
- Color-coded status columns and visual dashboards make project health visible instantly without training
- Automations builder lets non-technical users create if-then workflows (when status changes, notify someone, move item) without code
- Dashboard widgets aggregate data across multiple boards for management-level reporting
Cons
- Minimum seat requirements on paid plans (3 seats on Standard) inflate costs for very small teams
- Becomes cluttered quickly when teams create too many boards without establishing naming conventions and archival practices
Visual-First Design
Monday.com's core insight is that most people process visual information faster than text. Status columns use colors (green for done, yellow for in progress, red for stuck) that communicate project health at a glance. The board layout resembles a spreadsheet, which lowers the learning curve for teams accustomed to tracking work in Excel or Google Sheets. Dashboards aggregate data from multiple boards into widgets (charts, numbers, timelines) that provide a management-level view without clicking into individual items. This visual approach works exceptionally well for client-facing project tracking and executive reporting.
Automations and Integrations
The automations builder uses a natural language interface: 'When status changes to Done, notify team lead and move item to Completed group.' Non-technical users can create these without assistance, which removes the bottleneck of needing an admin to configure workflows. Monday.com offers over 200 automation recipes covering notifications, status changes, item creation, date adjustments, and cross-board mirroring. The integration marketplace includes connections to Slack, Gmail, Google Drive, Salesforce, and HubSpot, making it viable as a central work hub for operations teams.
$9/user/month Basic, $12/user/month Standard, $19/user/month Pro
ClickUp
Best Value
Best for: Teams wanting maximum features at the lowest price
“ClickUp tries to be everything for everyone, and it largely succeeds on features while struggling on polish. It offers every view type (list, board, timeline, Gantt, calendar, mind map, whiteboard), built-in docs, goals, time tracking, and dashboards at $7/user/month. The trade-off is that the interface is dense and the performance is inconsistent.”
Pros
- Most features per dollar of any PM tool: time tracking, docs, goals, whiteboards, and every view type included at the base price
- Customizable at every level (space, folder, list) with custom fields, statuses, and automations per project
- 20+ million users and active development mean features ship constantly
Cons
- Interface complexity overwhelms new users; the sheer number of options creates decision fatigue during setup
- Performance inconsistency: pages occasionally load slowly, and the mobile app is notably less reliable than the desktop experience
Feature Breadth
ClickUp's feature list reads like the union of every competing product's feature list. List view, board view, timeline, Gantt chart, calendar, table, mind map, and whiteboard. Built-in document editor. Goal tracking with OKR support. Native time tracking. Sprints. Forms. Custom dashboards. The question is never 'can ClickUp do this?' but rather 'how well does ClickUp do this?' For teams that cannot justify multiple tool subscriptions, the breadth is real value. A startup paying $7/user/month gets capabilities that would cost $30-50/user/month by combining specialized tools.
Customization Depth
ClickUp allows different configurations at every level of its hierarchy (workspace, space, folder, list). Each project can have its own custom statuses, custom fields, automations, and view configurations. This flexibility is powerful for organizations managing diverse work types (engineering sprints, marketing campaigns, client projects) within a single tool. The downside is that without governance, workspaces become inconsistent, making cross-project reporting unreliable. Organizations that succeed with ClickUp typically designate a workspace admin who maintains templates and enforces conventions.
Migration and Adoption
ClickUp provides importers for Jira, Asana, Trello, Monday.com, Basecamp, and Todoist, which reduces the friction of switching. The import quality varies: Jira imports preserve most metadata, while imports from less structured tools lose some context. The learning curve is steep for the first two weeks as teams navigate the dense interface and decide which features to use. ClickUp's documentation and university courses help, but organizations should budget for a 2-4 week onboarding period before teams reach productivity parity with their previous tool.

Jira
Best for Enterprise
Best for: Large software teams with mature Agile processes
“Jira remains the default choice for large software organizations, and that is both a recommendation and a warning. It handles sprints, backlogs, roadmaps, and cross-team dependencies at a scale no competitor matches. It also carries 20 years of accumulated complexity that makes simple tasks harder than they should be. Teams over 200 engineers will likely end up on Jira. Teams under 50 should consider alternatives seriously.”
Pros
- Agile workflow support (scrum boards, sprint planning, backlog grooming, velocity charts) is the most mature and configurable available
- Scales to thousands of users with advanced permissions, custom workflows, and cross-project dependency tracking
- Free tier for up to 10 users makes it accessible for small teams that expect to grow into enterprise scale
Cons
- Performance and interface complexity have become the industry's go-to example of enterprise software bloat
- Configuration requires a dedicated Jira admin; teams without one end up with poorly structured projects and unusable dashboards
Agile at Scale
Jira's core strength is its Agile workflow engine. Scrum boards with sprint planning, backlog management, and velocity tracking work well for individual teams. Where Jira differentiates is at scale: cross-project boards that show work spanning multiple teams, Advanced Roadmaps (Premium tier) that roll up team-level plans into program-level timelines, and dependency tracking that surfaces blockers across organizational boundaries. For organizations practicing SAFe, LeSS, or other scaled Agile frameworks, Jira provides the structural support these methodologies require.
Atlassian Ecosystem
Jira's value multiplies within the Atlassian ecosystem. Confluence provides documentation linked to Jira issues. Bitbucket connects code changes to tickets. Jira Service Management extends workflows to IT and customer support. Statuspage communicates incidents. Atlas provides team and project directories. For organizations committed to the Atlassian stack, this integration depth is unmatched. The trade-off is vendor lock-in: migrating away from a deeply integrated Atlassian deployment is a multi-month project.
The Configuration Problem
Jira's flexibility is its most dangerous feature. Every organization configures it differently, which means best practices are hard to transfer, consultants charge premium rates for Jira administration, and new hires spend weeks learning the local Jira conventions. The healthiest Jira deployments use the fewest custom issue types, the simplest workflows, and the most restrictive permissions. If your organization is evaluating Jira, designate an admin from day one and give them the authority to say no to customization requests.
Free (up to 10 users), $7.75/user/month Standard, $13.50/user/month Premium
Basecamp
Honorable Mention
Best for: Async-first remote teams that want simplicity
“Basecamp is the anti-Jira. It has a fixed feature set (to-dos, message boards, schedules, docs, chat), a flat monthly price ($299 for unlimited users), and strong opinions about how teams should communicate (async by default, meetings as a last resort). Teams that share these values love it. Teams that need Gantt charts, sprints, or custom workflows will not find them here.”
Pros
- Flat $299/month for unlimited users means cost per user drops as the team grows, making it extremely cost-effective at 50+ people
- Async-first design with message boards, automatic check-ins, and Hill Charts reduces meeting dependency
- Deliberately simple feature set means near-zero onboarding time and no configuration overhead
Cons
- No timeline view, Gantt charts, sprint boards, or workload management for teams that need scheduling tools
- Reporting is minimal: no custom dashboards, velocity charts, or cross-project analytics
Async Communication Model
Basecamp structures communication around message boards (long-form, async discussion), automatic check-ins (scheduled questions like 'what did you work on today?'), and Campfire chat (real-time messaging when needed). This hierarchy makes async the default and synchronous the exception. For distributed teams across time zones, this design reduces the pressure to be online simultaneously and creates a searchable record of decisions. The Shape Up methodology (Basecamp's alternative to sprints) uses 6-week cycles with explicit cool-down periods, which many teams find more sustainable than 2-week sprints.
Hill Charts and Progress Tracking
Hill Charts are Basecamp's novel approach to progress tracking. Instead of percentage-complete bars (which are notoriously inaccurate), Hill Charts show whether work is in the 'figuring it out' phase (uphill) or the 'making it happen' phase (downhill). Team members manually update their position on the hill, which forces an honest assessment of progress. It sounds simple, but it communicates the qualitative state of work (stuck, exploring, executing, finishing) better than numerical progress indicators.
$299/month flat (unlimited users), $15/user/month (small teams)
Trello
Honorable Mention
Best for: Simple visual kanban for small teams and personal projects
“Trello invented the visual kanban board for project management and remains the simplest way to track work visually. Drag cards between columns, add checklists and due dates, and you are done. For teams of 2-15 people managing simple workflows, Trello's simplicity is its killer feature. For anything more complex, you will outgrow it quickly.”
Pros
- The simplest learning curve of any PM tool: most users are productive within 10 minutes of signing up
- Free tier is generous enough for small teams to use indefinitely without hitting meaningful limitations
- Power-Ups (integrations) extend functionality for teams that need specific capabilities without switching tools
Cons
- No native timeline, Gantt, workload, or reporting views, which limits utility for teams managing complex projects
- Boards become unwieldy beyond 50-100 cards, with no built-in way to archive, filter, or manage large volumes of work
Simplicity as a Feature
Trello's board-list-card hierarchy maps directly to how most people think about work: columns represent stages (To Do, In Progress, Done), and cards represent tasks that move between stages. There is no project hierarchy to configure, no sprint to plan, no velocity to track. You create a board, add lists, create cards, and start working. This simplicity means Trello gets used: the adoption rate is higher than complex tools because the barrier to participation is nearly zero. For freelancers, small teams, and personal project tracking, this simplicity is exactly the right amount of tool.
Power-Ups and Limitations
Power-Ups extend Trello's base functionality with calendar views, Gantt charts, time tracking, and integrations with other tools. The free tier includes unlimited Power-Ups, which is generous. However, adding Power-Ups to compensate for missing native features creates a patchwork experience that is less reliable than a tool with built-in capabilities. Calendar Power-Ups are not as good as Asana's native calendar. Gantt Power-Ups are not as good as Monday.com's native timeline. At some point, the accumulation of Power-Ups signals that you have outgrown Trello.
Free, $5/user/month Standard, $10/user/month Premium
Coda
Honorable Mention
Best for: Teams that want to build custom PM workflows in a doc-like interface
“Coda sits at the intersection of documents and applications. It looks like a doc but behaves like a database-powered app with buttons, automations, and formulas. Teams that enjoy building custom tools will find it liberating. Teams that want an off-the-shelf PM solution will find it frustrating, because Coda requires you to build (or adopt a template for) your own project management system.”
Pros
- Formulas, buttons, and automations let you build custom workflows that exactly match your process without writing code
- Packs (integrations) pull data from external tools (Jira, GitHub, Slack, Google Sheets) into your Coda doc for unified views
- Combines project tracking, documentation, and decision logs in a single living document
Cons
- Requires significant setup investment: you are building a PM tool, not using one out of the box
- Performance degrades on large docs with many cross-references, formulas, and embedded pack data
Docs-as-Apps Philosophy
Coda's fundamental concept is that a document should be able to do anything a custom application can do. Tables function as databases with relations, formulas, and views. Buttons trigger actions (send a Slack message, update a status, create a calendar event). Automations run on schedules or in response to data changes. This means you can build a sprint planning tool, a product launch checklist, or a hiring pipeline that works exactly the way your team works, without compromising on a generic tool's assumptions about your process.
Integration Packs
Coda Packs connect external data sources into your doc. Pull Jira issues, GitHub PRs, Google Calendar events, or Slack messages into Coda tables, then build views and automations on top of them. This creates a unified operational surface that aggregates data from multiple tools. The practical value is highest for teams that use many specialized tools and need a single view across all of them. The limitation is that Pack data syncs periodically (not real-time), so the unified view may lag behind the source tools by minutes to hours.

How we evaluated
Project management tools split into distinct categories that get compared as if they were interchangeable: issue trackers built for engineering teams (Linear, Jira), all-in-one workspaces (Notion, Coda), and dedicated task/project tools (Asana, Monday.com, ClickUp, Basecamp, Trello). This comparison weighs fit for the stated use case over feature-count.
Each tool was assessed on the dimensions that decide real outcomes, the same ones in the comparison table above:
- Team fit: whether the tool is built for engineering workflows, cross-functional teams, or general task tracking.
- Scalability: how the tool holds up as a team grows from a handful of people to hundreds, including performance and permission complexity.
- Pricing model: per-seat cost at small and large team sizes, and what's gated behind higher tiers.
- Workflow trade-offs: what you give up in exchange for the tool's core strength, since no tool is best at everything.
What we reviewed
This comparison draws on official documentation and publicly posted pricing, hands-on use, and aggregated third-party review data. It reflects the market as of 2026 and is refreshed as tools reprice or change plans materially.
Editorial independence: this is a vendor-neutral comparison with no paid placements, sponsorships, or affiliate links. Rankings reflect fit for the stated use cases, not commercial relationships.
