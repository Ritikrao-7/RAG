#2: Trello — best for creating and executing project roadmaps
Trello is a solid choice for visualizing and executing project roadmaps in Agile settings. Thanks to its highly visual nature, it’s easy for both you and stakeholders to get a bird’s-eye view of the project and know exactly where it stands.
Why choose Trello?
POV: You and your team need a clear visual approach to project management that will help streamline your workflow.
Solution: Trello’s signature Kanban view can help you with that.
| Trello’s pros | Trello’s cons | 
|---|---|
| – Customizable – Intuitive drag-and-drop interface – AI writing assistant | – Cluttered interface – Lack of some advanced features – Scalability issues | 
I found collaborating in Trello pretty smooth, as it allows you to:
- Break down the tasks from the project roadmap on different boards.
- Assign labels and people.
- Add files, links, comments.
Trello’s Power-Ups (integrations) allow you to connect the boards so that whatever your team does on their board automatically updates your roadmap. They are available in the free plan, which is great for small teams that want to test the waters before investing in an app.
For smoother communication with your teammates, there’s an AI writing assistant available too, which helps summarize or improve your writing, fix spelling, change tone, etc.
And as far as customization goes, Trello didn’t disappoint me. It offers plenty of options, including adding a checklist to your card, which I found especially helpful, as it helps me not forget any of the steps in my workflow.
Additionally, Trello offers plenty of automation options, one of which is the card button option. When you create a card button, you select what you want the button to do, from moving the card to another space and adding or removing assignees, to leaving a comment or checking the task off a list.
Aside from the checklists, Trello enables you to add other options to your cards, thanks again to Power-Ups. However, while it may be handy for an app to present a ton of information, it can become a bit overwhelming — at least it was for me. So, if you’re more inclined to minimalism, you may want to dial down on the available options.
Another thing to consider is that Trello might not be so easily scalable. It is useful in smaller, Agile project teams. But, I can easily imagine it getting too cluttered if a larger team were to use it for a bigger project, especially in a Waterfall project management setting.
What’s new in Trello?
Recent Trello changes include a dedicated Inbox for capturing tasks from Siri voice recordings or email messages, a Planner for mapping out your agenda visually, and Mirror Cards that ensure updates stay in sync across several boards at once.

#3: Smartsheet — best for users who want Excel, but better
You can think of Smartsheet as a significantly enhanced version of Excel or Google Sheets. While the software also offers Kanban, Calendar, and List views, its main appeal lies in its Grid (Spreadsheet) view.
Why choose Smartsheet?
POV: You work in a small team that’s a fan of Excel spreadsheets and is wondering if there’s something slightly better (or less glitchy!).
Solution: Smartsheet’s what you need.
| Smartsheet’s pros | Smartsheet’s cons | 
|---|---|
| – Fully customizable – Easy to use – Efficient communication in real time | – Prone to human error – Steep learning curve | 
Smartsheet is highly customizable — if you can do it in Excel or Sheets, you can do it in Smartsheet, but better.
In addition to supporting many of the same formulas as Excel and Sheets, it also lets you:
- Create reports.
- Connect and view this information in Gantt or Calendar views.
- Create project dependencies and determine the critical path.
- Collaborate with your team in real time (directly in the sheet).
- Set project baselines.
I especially liked the project baseline feature, as it helps track baselines, actual dates, and the schedule variance between them. This gives you an accurate insight into your project performance, without you having to waste your time constantly checking each task on your list — a huge deal for small teams that might already have a lot on their plates.
All this makes Smartsheet great for project managers who like the customizability and layout of spreadsheets, but need more focused PM functionalities built into their software.
On the other hand, the amount of formatting required to create a functioning project management system (much like in Excel or Google Sheets) is Smartsheet’s biggest flaw. If you’re not already well-versed in these tools, using Smartsheet is going to be a major obstacle.
Moreover, just like in Excel, a single mistake somewhere in your sheets or formulas could create errors and potentially botch much of your data.

One of the main reasons Zoho Projects is great for traditional, Waterfall-type projects is because of the way it handles project dependencies.

Zoho also allows you to add lag time for activities that need it or in case you want to add buffers to your schedule. All of this is fairly easy to do in the Gantt view, allowing you to effectively manage your project timeline and critical path.
In Zoho Projects, you can communicate with your coworkers in the comments section too. #11: Basecamp — Best for simple flat-rate pricing
Instead of offering endless customization options, automations, and complex workflows, Basecamp focuses on keeping everything simple.
If your small team wants one place for tasks, discussions, files, and schedules, without spending hours configuring the software, Basecamp is a good choice.
Why choose Basecamp?
POV: Your team spends more time managing the project management software than actually managing projects.
Solution: Basecamp provides a straightforward workspace where everyone knows where to find what they need.
| Basecamp’s pros | Basecamp’s cons | 
|---|---|
| – Clean, intuitive interface – Built-in team communication tools – Easy to learn and adopt – Flat-rate pricing option for larger teams – Excellent organization of project resources | – Limited customization – No Gantt charts or advanced reporting – Lacks some automation features | 
One of the first things I noticed while using Basecamp is how little time it takes to get comfortable with it. Unlike many project management platforms that present dozens of menus and settings, Basecamp keeps the interface clean and focuses on the essentials.
Each project has its own dedicated workspace that brings together everything the team needs, including task lists, message boards, schedules, files, and real-time group chat. I found this especially helpful because it reduces the need to jump between multiple apps just to find project information or ask a quick question.
To-do lists are straightforward to create and organize. You can assign tasks, set due dates, add comments, and attach files. This means that automatic check-ins help managers gather updates without constantly scheduling status meetings. For small teams juggling multiple responsibilities, this can save a surprising amount of time.
Another feature I appreciated is Basecamp’s emphasis on communication. Instead of relying heavily on email, teammates can use message boards for announcements, Campfire for group chat, and comments within tasks to keep conversations tied to specific work. This makes it much easier to follow project discussions without searching through lengthy email threads.
That said, Basecamp’s simplicity comes with trade-offs. If you’re looking for advanced project planning tools like Gantt charts, workload management, custom workflows, or sophisticated reporting, you’ll probably find Basecamp too limiting.
Also, you are limited to only one project in the free plan. For teams running multiple projects at once, Basecamp’s free plan won’t be useful.
In light of that, I found that while Basecamp works very well for straightforward project management, it becomes less suitable for teams managing highly complex projects with multiple dependencies or detailed reporting requirements. In those situations, a more feature-rich platform may be a better fit.

Which project management tool is best for your small team
To narrow down your choices instantly, look at your team through three lenses: your team’s size, your budget, and how you prefer to organize work.
Check your team size
- Micro-Teams (2–5 users) need zero setup friction.
- Mid-Sized Small Teams (6–10 users) require structured accountability.
- Scaling Small Teams (11–20 users) need heavy collaboration features without getting chaotic.
Set your budget
- The $0 Free Tier — you want full functionality without a monthly bill.
- Paid ($5 to $10+ per user) — you have the cash flow to pay for advanced features or strict data formatting.
Identify your workflow style
- Visual/Kanban — you like dragging cards across columns (like a digital whiteboard).
- Docs-Heavy — you need a digital filing cabinet for text, wikis, and notes.
- Traditional/Spreadsheet — you live in rows, formulas, and rigid timelines.
