Hands-on test: monday.com vs Asana for client approvals
To compare how monday.com and Asana handle a common small-team workflow, I modeled the same client-approval project in both platforms. The test uses five participants: a project manager, creator, internal reviewer, department lead, and external client.
The workflow covers eight stages, from receiving the client brief through internal review and client approval to final project closeout. I compared setup effort, external-client permissions, approval steps, and plan requirements.
| Test | monday.com | Asana | 
|---|---|---|
| Project setup time | 18 minutes | 14 minutes | 
| External client access | Client could view and update assigned items on a Shareable Board | Client could access assigned project work as a guest | 
| Approval process | Used status changes and automation to route work for review | Used a dedicated Approval task with approve, reject, or request changes options | 
| Changes requested | Status returned the item to the creator and triggered a notification | Request changes returned the task to the assignee with reviewer feedback | 
| Lowest suitable plan | Standard | Advanced | 
| Guest cost for one client | No additional charge for one guest | No additional guest charge | 
| Main restriction observed | Approval flow required configuring statuses and automation rules | Native Approval tasks required a higher plan than basic guest collaboration | 
What the test showed
Asana required less configuration for the approval step because the reviewer could respond directly with an approval decision rather than relying on a custom status. In this sample workflow, the project took about four fewer minutes to configure, and the request-changes process was easier to understand from the task itself.
monday.com gave me more control over how the approval process moved between stages, but I had to configure the status values and automation rules first. I would favor monday.com when a small team wants to customize the approval process around its existing workflow, while Asana is easier to follow when the main requirement is a defined review and approval sequence.
Sample test observations
monday.com: The external client only needed access to the Shareable Board used for the project, which kept unrelated internal boards outside the client workspace. The main setup work involved creating approval statuses and deciding which changes should trigger notifications or reassignment.
Asana: Guest access required little additional setup once the client was added to the project. The dedicated Approval task made the decision point explicit, although using that native workflow raised the minimum plan required for this comparison.

How to choose project management software
The best project management software for a small team should match your current workload and remain affordable as the team grows. Before comparing feature lists, check what the software will cost at your actual headcount and which plan limits are most likely to affect your work.
1. Calculate the price at your actual team size
Start with the number of people who will need paid access, then check minimum seat requirements and billing increments. A $9-per-user plan may cost more than expected if the vendor sells seats in blocks or requires a minimum number of users.
2. Identify your signal to upgrade
Review what the free or entry-level plan excludes and decide which restriction your team is most likely to reach first. Common upgrade triggers include project limits, advanced reporting, workload planning, or access to more automation.
3. Count everyone who needs access
Include employees as well as freelancers, contractors, and clients who may need to view or update project work. Some platforms provide free guest access, while others require paid seats for external collaborators or restrict what guests can do.
4. Decide who will maintain the software
More configurable platforms usually require someone to manage custom fields and workflow rules. Permissions, templates, and automations may also need regular updates as projects change, so consider whether a project manager or team lead has time to handle that work.
5. Test one real project before migrating
Run the same active project in your top two choices before moving the rest of your work. Compare the setup process, whether team members can easily find assigned tasks, and how many manual updates are still required. Any missing capability that affects deadlines or handoffs should weigh more heavily than minor interface preferences.
FAQs
What is the best project management software for a small team?
monday.com is the best project management software for most small teams because it combines visual project tracking with workflow automation and straightforward setup. ClickUp is better for teams that want more task management capabilities at a lower price, while Asana suits teams that prioritize ease of use.
What is the best free project management software for a small team?
ClickUp offers one of the most feature-rich free plans for small teams, with unlimited members and tasks. However, storage is limited to 60MB and some features have usage caps. Jira supports up to 10 users for free, while Trello allows up to 10 collaborators and 10 open boards per Workspace.
How much should a small team pay for project management software?
Small teams can expect to pay about $4 to $12 per user per month for many entry-level paid plans with annual billing. Actual costs depend on headcount and minimum-seat rules. Zoho Projects starts at $4, ClickUp at $7, monday.com at $9, and Asana Starter at $10.99 per user monthly.
Does a small team need project management software?
A small team may not need dedicated software when a shared spreadsheet or task list provides enough visibility. Project management software becomes ideal when team members need to track ownership and dependencies across several projects, especially when recurring deadlines become difficult to manage through spreadsheets or chat.
